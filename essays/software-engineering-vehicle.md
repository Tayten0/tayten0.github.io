## Introduction

The term "Software Engineering" is often thrown around as a catch-all term by people discussing computer science as a career or area of study, but saying you want to be a software engineer is about as specific as saying you want to do math for a living. Computer science—and by extension, software engineering—can be applied across almost every field of work and study. It simply depends on how you want to apply it. Do you want to develop a program that could allow a fighter jet to take in data from its radar and convert that to visual information for its pilot to use? Maybe you want to create a piece of software that'll help the next Mars rover navigate through the hazardous terrain of the red planet, or maybe you want to create a program that measures bacteria levels in a controlled environment to help with a biological study? Maybe you want to do something a bit simpler and help a business sustain its online operations by updating its website and database systems. Whatever it is, software engineering isn't the area of expertise, it's the tool you use to expand your capabilities in it. With that in mind, I wanted to take a step back and think about what areas I'd be interested in developing my software engineering skills in and outline the skills and experiences I hope to develop in the future.  

## Game Development

<img src="../img/software-engineering-vehicle/game-development.png" width="450" alt="The final boss level of my game, Climbing a Mountain: The Game.">  
<i>Figure 1: The final boss level of my game, "Climbing a Mountain: The Game."</i>   

A video game isn't just several thousand lines of code packed into a single file. It's a complex program that tells the art assets, the music, and the gameplay how all three areas should interact, akin to how a composer manages an orchestra. And just like an orchestra, you need every instrument—every component—for the game to work properly. Video games are something I've loved since I was a kid. From playing simple web games on the PC we had at home, to playing split-screen survival and FPS games with my brother on our PS4, to co-op games with my friends, video games have been a key part of my life. As I've grown older, my curiosity into how these games were created has only grown. I've had some introductory experiences with game development in high school, having made a couple of simple web games both solo and with my friends, but that's nowhere close to a full, proper indie game project. I'd like to learn how to utilize a proper game engine like Unity or Unreal Engine. I'd like to learn how to create my own custom character models—whether 2-D or 3-D—and make them controllable in-game. Truth be told, the music and soundtrack department aren't areas I'd be particularly interested in learning, and would probably work with a third-party like a friend to create, but I'd still like to learn how these soundtracks are implemented into the game. How do you make music loop during certain gameplay segments? How do you create triggers for sound cues and music to play once a player reaches a certain point? The whole video game development process is broad and requires a lot of components to be created before the software can tie it all together, and it's something that I'd be interested in looking into in the future.  

## Finance

<img src="../img/software-engineering-vehicle/finance.png" width="450" alt="Stock image of a generic candlestick chart.">  
<i>Figure 2: Stock image of a generic candlestick chart. Source: <a href="https://www.ig.com/au/share-trading/what-are-shares" target="_blank">IG Australia</a></i>  

Even if you've never been interested in finance or stocks in general, I'm sure many of you heard of the GameStop short squeeze that occurred back in 2021, when a large group of Reddit users all bought large quantities of GameStop shares in order to drive up the price of the stock and create huge financial losses for hedge funds and short sellers. Up to that point, I had nothing but a basic understanding of stocks: you buy and sell shares of a company—which brings a proportional amount of ownership over the company—and the value of this share can go up or down depending on the company's performance. It was the GameStop short squeeze that sparked my curiosity into stocks, but it wasn't until my friend began doing some minor day-trading during our senior year of high school that I did something with that curiosity and actually started to learn about stocks. Since then, I've opened my own stock account and have put together my own portfolio, but my knowledge about this subject is still elementary at best. I'd like to learn more about stocks—strategies and figures that analysts use to predict the performance of a stock, what they look for when doing research on companies to invest in, and just general good-to-know basics. Once I have this foundational knowledge, I'd like to learn how software engineering has been integrated into this process, and how to do it myself. Whether that's by designing a program to comb through financial reports or autonomously executing trades by using the strategies I mentioned earlier.  

## Artificial Intelligence

<img src="../img/software-engineering-vehicle/artificial-intelligence.png" width="450" alt="Logo of Google Gemini.">  
<i>Figure 3: Logo of Google Gemini. Source: <a href="https://gemini.google/about/" target="_blank">Google Gemini</a></i>  

Artificial intelligence is the big new thing to happen in tech. It's quite controversial, and understandably so. Companies around the world have cumulatively spent over a trillion dollars in AI development and investments, but the immediate results raise valid concerns. We see thousands of workers—from artists to software engineers—laid off and replaced by automation, while the vast new data centers made to feed this AI demand strain local utility prices and water resources. Meanwhile, online spaces are increasingly flooded with low-quality 'AI slop' content used to farm engagement, alongside the rise of harmful deepfakes. And last but certainly not least, it poses serious challenges to academic integrity as students become increasingly reliant on AI to complete their assignments for them. Despite this, I believe that AI can be used for more than just corporate cost-cutting measures, and there's proof of this. AI has been used by doctors to <a href="https://www.alation.com/blog/ai-healthcare-breakthroughs-2025-innovations/" target="_blank">help diagnose patients</a>, by researchers to <a href="https://hai.stanford.edu/news/how-ai-is-transforming-scientific-discovery-while-keeping-humans-at-the-center" target="_blank">develop new antibodies and treatments for diseases</a>, by engineers trying to <a href="https://hai.stanford.edu/news/how-ai-is-transforming-scientific-discovery-while-keeping-humans-at-the-center" target="_blank">optimize magnetic fields in nuclear fusion reactors</a>, and so much more. I'm interested in focusing on this side of AI. I want to learn how LLMs like ChatGPT and Google Gemini work, how we can make them more efficient and less resource intensive, how researchers have implemented AI to do the good that they have done, and look into ways I could implement AI into my own projects to actually help people.
